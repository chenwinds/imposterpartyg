Imposter Game Questions: 60+ Ways to Catch the Liar

A good imposter game question does two jobs at once: it gives away nothing about the secret word, and it forces the imposter to invent a specific answer they cannot check. Vague questions ("Is it nice?") teach you nothing; narrow questions ("Does it bark?") hand the imposter the answer. Every question below sits in the sweet spot. New to the game? Read the full rules first, then come back with your group.

Top 10 safe opening questions

Use these in the first round when nobody has any information. They are safe for civilians and quietly stressful for the imposter:

  1. How often do you use or encounter this? Imposters love answering "every day" - which is instantly wrong for words like Skis or Passport.
  2. Where would you usually find it? Forces a location guess: indoors, outdoors, kitchen, garage?
  3. Is it bigger than a microwave? Size comparisons are brutal when you are guessing blind.
  4. Would most people in this room have one, or have done it? Separates everyday items from niche ones.
  5. Is it more of an indoor or an outdoor thing? Simple, fast, and hard to hedge twice in a row.
  6. Would a ten-year-old know what this is? Splits toys and cartoons from taxes and wine.
  7. Does it make any sound? Animals and machines do; furniture and feelings do not.
  8. Can you hold it in one hand? A physical trait that is almost impossible to bluff confidently.
  9. Is it expensive? "Depends" is the classic imposter answer - watch who says it first.
  10. Would you take it on an overnight trip? Plausibility questions catch imposters who are picturing the wrong object entirely.

10 trap questions for when you have a suspect

Once someone smells wrong, switch to questions that demand specific knowledge. Each one lists the tell to watch for:

  1. Say your clue again, using exactly three words. Bluffers can rarely compress a made-up clue on demand.
  2. What color is it, typically? If the word is Firetruck and they say blue, the round is over.
  3. Would you give this as a gift? A watch, yes. A tornado or a sidewalk, no.
  4. Does it need power, batteries, or charging? Technical guesses are coin flips for liars.
  5. Could you eat any part of it? The ultimate food-vs-everything-else check.
  6. Has it existed for more than a hundred years? Wheel, yes. iPhone, no. History questions expose guessing fast.
  7. If it broke, would fixing it cost more than dinner for two? Implied value is hard to estimate for an object you are inventing.
  8. Is it something you do alone or with others? Sleeping alone, tennis with others - verbs and activities crack here.
  9. Does it smell like anything? Flowers vs. garbage. Sensory questions are nearly impossible to fake.
  10. Describe it without repeating any word from your first clue. The imposter built their whole story on one clue - make them build a second.

Questions by word category

Match the question to the category your group is playing. (Need words? Our word generator has pairs in each of these categories.)

Food & drink words

  1. Would you need anything besides your hands to eat or drink it?
  2. Would its texture change if it sat on the table for an hour?
  3. Could you share it without cutting or pouring?
  4. Would you expect it at a breakfast buffet?
  5. Would you serve it to a guest without preparing it first?
  6. Is it eaten hot, cold, or both?
  7. Would kids ask for it by name?
  8. Does it show up at birthday parties?
  9. Could it be a full meal by itself?
  10. Would you find it in a school cafeteria?

Animal words

  1. Could you legally keep it as a pet where you live?
  2. Would it fit in your living room?
  3. Is it faster than you?
  4. Does it live in water for most of its life?
  5. Would you hear it before you see it?
  6. Is it dangerous to humans?
  7. Can it fly?
  8. Would you see it at a zoo?
  9. Does it appear in children's cartoons a lot?
  10. Could it beat a house cat in a fight?

Place words

  1. Would visitors usually stay there for more than an hour?
  2. Would rain change what people do there?
  3. Would you expect a queue at busy times?
  4. Do you need to plan ahead before going?
  5. Would you take something home after visiting?
  6. Is it free to enter?
  7. Would you go there alone?
  8. Is it louder or quieter than this room?
  9. Could you spend a whole day there?
  10. Is there one in every city?

Object words

  1. Would it fit inside a school bag?
  2. Would you expect to use it during a power cut?
  3. Could two people use it at the same time?
  4. Would getting it wet usually cause a problem?
  5. Do you own more than one?
  6. Has your phone replaced it, partly or completely?
  7. Would you lend it to a friend without worry?
  8. Is it older than the internet, as an invention?
  9. Would you find it in a kitchen drawer?
  10. Could you buy a decent one for under $20?

Pick questions for your scenario

Party icebreaker (adults, drinks, loud room): keep questions short and physical - size, sound, one-hand rules. Long questions die in a noisy room. Favor trap questions early; confident groups enjoy the aggression.

Family game with kids: stick to concrete things kids can picture ("Would it fit in a school bag?") and skip anything about price, work, or adult habits. Let kids ask their own questions even if they leak the word - the point is the arguing, not the verdict.

Online or voice-call round: connection lag looks exactly like a guilty pause. Ask one question at a time in a fixed order, and give everyone a ten-second "thinking is allowed" rule before anyone answers.

The three tiers of clues

Tier 1 - Too obvious: "It's a yellow fruit." (The imposter now knows the word. You've lost.)

Tier 2 - The sweet spot: "It shows up at breakfast more than dinner." (Fits banana, vaguely fits other things. The imposter sweats.)

Tier 3 - Too vague: "It's nice." (Proves nothing. You look like the imposter.)

Aim for Tier 2: a clue that's clearly true for your word, but that requires actually knowing the word to produce. Every question in this list is designed to test whether someone's clue really lives in Tier 2.

How to spot the imposter's clue

  • The echo: their clue is a remix of the previous player's.
  • The hedge: "It's kind of... a thing you... use?" Confidence gaps betray bluffers.
  • The overfit: their clue fits every word on the table. "It's fun!" helps nobody.

Picking good imposter topics

The word itself decides half the game before anyone speaks. Good imposter topics follow three rules:

  • Concrete beats abstract. "Toaster" produces great clues; "Freedom" produces philosophy class. Save abstract words for expert groups.
  • The pair must overlap. Coffee/Tea works because half the questions can't split them. Coffee/Airplane is over in ten seconds. Every category in our word list is built on pairs that survive round one.
  • Everyone at the table must know the word. Anime characters are a brilliant topic for an anime group and a disaster for a mixed family. When in doubt, food, animals, and household objects work for every group on earth.

Starter categories that never fail: food & drink, animals, places, household objects, jobs, sports. Niche categories (gaming, celebrities, 90s cartoons) are seasoning - use them once the group is warmed up.

FAQ

Can the imposter ask questions too?

Yes - everyone asks in turn order. If only confirmed civilians ask, the question order itself leaks the roles. The imposter's questions also quietly reveal what they think the word is.

Should questions be yes/no or open-ended?

Yes/no early, open-ended later. Quick binary answers are hard to fake consistently; open follow-ups are for the discussion phase when you want a suspect to explain a clue in their own words.

Can you ask "Is it pizza?" directly?

Only with a pre-agreed final-guess house rule. Naming a word turns clue-testing into lucky guessing - most groups ban it during questioning.

How many questions per round?

One per player is the standard rhythm. If your group keeps cracking the word too early, cut follow-ups rather than reaching for obscure vocabulary nobody enjoys.

What if the imposter guesses the secret word?

House rule. A popular variant lets a caught imposter steal the win by naming the word - which forces civilians to keep their clues vague. Decide before the round starts.
